# Systemic Technical Briefing

Use this reference when the Deck's main job is to make a complex technical domain understandable as a whole: its state, relationships, capabilities, standards, ecosystem, or direction. It can guide an internal briefing, public technical sharing, industry landscape, platform overview, or similar presentation for any audience.

Do not use it merely because the topic is technical or the sources contain architecture diagrams. Prefer another direction when the primary job is a product reveal, scientific argument, hands-on tutorial, code or mechanism deep dive, incident review, detailed implementation review, or single-object demonstration. An individual page in those Decks may still use the ordinary diagram guidance when a real relationship needs explanation.

This is conditional taste guidance inside the existing communication contract, visual direction, Page Briefs, composition plan, and QA. It does not create a style profile, fixed scenario list, page-order template, schema field, media quota, or second design specification.

## Desired result

Aim for a presentation that feels systematic, evidence-rich, technically credible, and easy to navigate.

- Let the audience find the page's main responsibility and first focal point quickly, then discover deeper structure and evidence.
- Treat richness as high semantic or evidence density with low visual-search cost, not as more boxes, arrows, logos, screenshots, or small labels.
- Build authority through clear relationships, trustworthy material, and precise interpretation rather than institutional decoration or generic technology texture.
- Keep one coherent visual grammar, but derive palette, typography, background, material treatment, and identity from the actual topic, brand, audience, and viewing context. Do not imitate a fixed white-blue-orange skin.
- Allow different page purposes and densities. A Deck may use evolution, current-state evidence, landscape, capability, scenario, standard, trend, roadmap, or implication pages in any useful combination; do not force a fixed sequence or require every role.

Determine the delivery mode from the communication contract. For a live presentation, keep the main canvas readable at presentation distance. When the artifact must also support later inspection, retain deeper material through adjacent detail pages, appendix content, presenter notes, or useful Slidev states. Never justify unreadable main-slide detail as a handout layer.

## Prefer a visual model when a relationship exists

When a page depends on sequence, hierarchy, flow, comparison, causality, containment, scope, change over time, or a system relationship, let an appropriate visual model carry the explanation instead of retreating to paragraphs. Do not invent a diagram when the content has no honest visual relationship or when a concrete object, quotation, or concise statement is stronger.

Give one primary model enough scale to lead the page. Use the title, direct labels, local interpretation, and a small number of visible anchors to establish what the audience should notice and how the presenter can trace the explanation. Treat anchor count and occupied area as compositional judgment, not a quota. Supporting evidence must clarify or qualify the same page responsibility rather than compete as another center.

Choose the model from the question it must answer. An industry landscape shows categories and positions; a capability map shows scope and layers; a reference architecture shows boundaries and dependencies; a flow or topology shows movement and interaction; a matrix shows coverage or maturity; a roadmap shows change. Do not call every complex visual an architecture diagram, and do not let a capability map imply deployment detail it does not contain.

## Make complex visuals readable

Use overview and focus when the complete model cannot remain readable at the intended viewing distance:

1. preserve an overview that makes the main regions, relationship, and reading path clear;
2. reveal or present the relevant layer, path, or decision at useful scale through emphasis, zoom, an adjacent detail page, or another behavior with genuine understanding value;
3. keep the complete static, reduced-motion, print, and offline results understandable without depending on hover, autoplay, or hidden transition-only content.

Do not shrink labels merely to keep every detail on one page. Preserve the full model in a later detail page or appendix when it remains useful, subject to any explicit page-count or source-fidelity constraint.

Treat an external diagram according to its role:

- When the original figure is evidence, retain its source and meaning, give it useful scale, and crop or annotate only enough to establish the current reading path.
- When only one region provides context, isolate that region and keep it subordinate to the primary explanation.
- When the source merely informs a clearer explanation, reconstruct the needed relationship in the Deck's visual grammar without inventing or silently changing technical meaning.
- When accurate reconstruction is not possible, preserve the original as a sourced reference or appendix item rather than pretending to understand missing detail.

Reject screenshot collages, logo walls, word clouds, or generic technical diagrams when they serve mainly as prestige or complexity signals. Two visuals share a page only when their comparison, causality, or sequence is explicit.

## Final rendered checks

The following failures block approval:

- the primary model, required labels, or reading path is not legible at the intended viewing distance;
- the model does not answer a clear page question or implies a false relationship;
- colors, shapes, boundaries, or connectors change meaning without a content reason;
- an external figure remains unreadable, untraceable, misleadingly cropped, or visually present without a defined evidence role;
- required understanding exists only in motion, hover, or another unavailable state;
- detail was made small merely to preserve the appearance of richness.

During aesthetic review, also ask:

- With secondary detail ignored, can the audience still find the page's responsibility, first focal point, and main relationship?
- Can the presenter explain the page by following a short, visible path through the model?
- If another equally complex diagram could replace this one without changing the argument, is the current visual only technical texture?
- Do full-size pages and the contact sheet show purposeful evidence peaks and recovery moments instead of a continuous wall of dense diagrams?

Treat these questions as rendered judgment, not a score, ratio, node limit, layout count, or mandatory title formula. Repair the highest-impact failed page and recheck it in the complete sequence.
