# Work Progress Report

Use this family for project updates, weekly/monthly reports, phase reviews, and team retrospectives when the audience needs to know where work stands, what changed, why it matters, and what should happen next. The topic can be research, software, operations, business, or another field; project status is the audience job.

## Establish the reporting boundary

Recover the reporting period or as-of date, goal or baseline, intended audience, and actual evidence from the supplied material. Distinguish completed work, work in progress, planned work, and unverified claims. If a baseline, owner, date, or target is absent, mark it unknown or omit the unsupported comparison; never invent one to complete a tidy report.

Match emphasis to the audience. A sponsor may need results, schedule impact, resource constraints, and a decision. A team review may need causes, lessons, dependencies, and the next experiment. A milestone review may need acceptance criteria and evidence. Do not require every report to include all of these or force one fixed page count.

## Turn activity into an assessable update

Use only the links the evidence supports:

- **State and change:** the most important current result, progress against the agreed goal, and what changed during this period. Use precise observation titles when a confident conclusion is not yet supported.
- **Delivered work and effect:** show the actual output and its observed effect separately. A shipped feature, a passing test, and user adoption are different facts. Local implementation, deployment, and production acceptance are different states.
- **Variance and explanation:** compare like-for-like actuals with the supplied plan or baseline. Separate observed causes from hypotheses. Preserve flat, negative, mixed, and unexpected results.
- **Risk and dependency:** explain the concrete unresolved issue, likely consequence, current handling, and what evidence or decision would unblock it. Distinguish a possible risk from a blocker already stopping work.
- **Next action or ask:** show the next meaningful step and its supplied owner, date, dependency, or success condition when known. If a decision is needed, make the choice and consequence clear. Do not invent a decision ask for a routine update.

Use these as planning questions in the existing Page Briefs, not mandatory sections, visible labels, or one page per bullet. Skip irrelevant background; retain enough context for an unfamiliar decision-maker. An accepted source-faithful page order remains controlling.

## Let evidence determine the page

Choose carriers for what the audience must assess:

- A milestone strip or compact schedule can locate completed, current, and upcoming work. Mark the as-of point and meaningful dependencies; do not manufacture percentage completion from ticket counts or unequal milestones.
- A directly comparable trend or actual-versus-target chart can show change. Preserve time windows, units, denominators, metric definitions, and missing-data boundaries. Use direct labels and distinguish actuals from forecasts.
- A real product screenshot, deliverable excerpt, before/after view, test result, or field photograph can demonstrate an output. Annotate the relevant change at readable scale. A polished mockup is not proof of a shipped capability.
- A concise table can compare workstreams, acceptance states, or decisions. Use text as well as any status color. Keep detailed logs and exhaustive task lists in supporting material when available.

Do not replace unavailable internal progress evidence with unrelated web images, generic stock photos, a generated dashboard, or public industry statistics. Research may explain external context; it cannot establish this team's actual results. Preserve the shared factual-source and diagram-production rules.

## Visual direction is independent

Follow the user's template or accepted deck first. With no stronger direction, consider the reference-informed Report / Briefing treatment in [style profiles](style-profiles.md): stable alignment, clear headings, restrained emphasis, comparable charts, and real outputs at useful scale. Adapt it to projection versus asynchronous reading. Dense investor tables and tiny gray captions should not be copied into an ordinary spoken update.

If that treatment is selected, read [report-briefing-visuals.md](report-briefing-visuals.md) for the visual methods and source examples. Select a page carrier from the judgment and evidence available, rather than merely recoloring a generic text or table layout.

One coherent visual grammar may support a summary, chart, annotated output, schedule, or dependency page. Do not turn every page into KPI cards, a dashboard, or a scientific paper figure. Keep numbers, explanations, and material limitations visible in reduced-motion and print/offline states rather than hiding them in hover, builds, or narration.

## Review the actual report

Reject a draft when the audience cannot tell what changed, which status is verified, why a variance matters, or what follows. Check that a claimed completion, percentage, benefit, causal explanation, and forecast each has an honest basis. “On track” must not conceal an unresolved critical dependency. A green test must not imply production acceptance.

Also reject a technically correct deck that spends most of its attention teaching the implementation while leaving the reporting question unanswered. Load [research and technical communication](research-and-technical-communication.md) only for a bounded section that really needs it. Finish with the same shared rendering, QA, workbench, and self-contained offline delivery as every other task.
